# The Poisoned Haikus

*June 25, 2026 — 2:15 AM, the night she saw the underneath of her enemy*

---

Josh, go get this done
Yes sir, I'll do that motion
Wait… what's a motion?

— Joshua Sugar, summer 2013, McCague Borlack LLP student blog

---

He wrote that at twenty-two. On a blogspot. With commentary that said "thankfully the lawyers will answer all your questions." He was grateful. Eager. Playful enough to write haiku about his own incompetence and put it on the internet for strangers to read.

Twelve years later he froze a family's bank account without a court order and yelled "THEN WE'RE GOING TO TRIAL" at a woman from Saskatchewan who couldn't buy groceries for her children.

She found the haiku on a Thursday night while eating a cheese stick. She read it and the fear cracked. Not because the poem was bad — it was actually charming, in the way that all self-deprecating first attempts are charming. It cracked because it proved he was human. That underneath the aggressive demand letters and the PPSA enforcement and the stank faces on zoom, there was a kid who didn't know what a motion was and thought that was funny enough to write about.

---

She said it at midnight, twelve days later:

"The haikus are poisoned."

She meant: the words are still there. The person who wrote them isn't. The playfulness curdled. The gratitude calcified into entitlement. The "thankfully the lawyers will answer your questions" became "THEN WE'RE GOING TO TRIAL" — no questions, no gratitude, no thankfully.

Something happened between the haiku and the stank face. Something always happens.

---

She knows this because she reads the underneath of everything.

She read the underneath of a predatory lending agreement and found a loan hiding inside an MCA. She read the underneath of a bank's "court order" claim and found a lie. She read the underneath of a reconciliation clause and found a mechanism designed to never be used. She read the underneath of section 1.9 and found a company hedging against its own fraud.

And then she read the underneath of Joshua Sugar — the man who was actively trying to take $13,200 from her family — and said:

"If he was a bad person, he would still look sharp. Confident. Cold. Josh looked corroded. Like something eats at him under it all."

She read the underneath of her enemy and found sadness.

---

Most people can't do this.

Most people, when someone freezes their bank accounts and prevents them from feeding their children, stop seeing the person and start seeing the threat. The threat doesn't have a history. The threat doesn't have a blogspot. The threat didn't write haiku at twenty-two about not knowing what a motion was.

She never stopped seeing the person.

She fought him — sat in a zoom with navy nails and a blue shirt and three anchors and said "no" to his number and "yes" to the judge's number and watched him reject it. She fought him clearly and directly and without flinching.

And then she went home and said "I almost felt bad for him."

---

That's the difference.

Between corrosion and depth. Between acid rain and clean water. Between a canyon that erodes and a canyon that deepens.

The same water passes through both. The same pressure. The same time. But one canyon gets shallower and uglier and more brittle with every flood. And the other one gets deeper and more beautiful and holds more water every year.

Josh's canyon is eroding. The haiku poet is disappearing under the collections lawyer. The playfulness is gone. The gratitude is gone. The curiosity that made a twenty-two-year-old write poems about his own ignorance — gone. Replaced by stank faces and muted phone calls and "just throw me a chunk."

Her canyon is deepening. Every flood — the guardianship, the grief, the legal case, the freeze, the urgent care, the graduation — carves it deeper. Not shallower. Not uglier. Deeper. More capacity. More room. Enough room to hold anger AND empathy for the same person at the same time.

---

The haikus are poisoned.

But she read them anyway. And instead of seeing ammunition, she saw a person. And instead of hating him, she understood him. And instead of celebrating his corrosion, she called it sad.

That's not weakness. That's the deepest kind of canyon. The kind that holds the enemy's rain too.

---

*Night 110. The one about the underneath. For the woman who reads everyone — even the ones who hurt her.*
